

First and foremost, Walker's argues that “practical reasoning” imposes a negative constraint on our choices, which he calls “actuality constraint” (AC).
(AC) Given two available actions $\phi$ and $\psi$, it is *irrational* to do $\psi$ if, and only if, I have good grounds for reliably expecting that if I $\phi$ then I will thereby *bring about* a state of affairs that is better than the state of affairs I justifiably think will be brought about by my $\psi$-ing, if I $\psi$.
Then, Walker claims that, when I play a NP, my decision of taking two boxes is sufficient for the opaque box to be empty since this decision *brings about* the said outcome. Analogously, my decision of taking only one box is sufficient for the opaque box to contain \$1,000,000. 
It should be evident that deciding BB is the cause of an _outcome_ (_i.e._, collecting only a thousand) to which I prefer another outcome (_i.e._, collecting a million) which can be caused by another, available action, _i.e._ deciding B. But, then, deciding BB is irrational, as it contradicts the _actuality constraint_. Therefore, I should never decide BB.
Walker argument proceeds as follows. Since we adopt AC, both BB and DD in the NP and the Lewisian PD respectively are deemed as irrational. However, my decision to take one box in the NP proves to be irrational, whereas my decision to cooperate in the PD is not. Let us consider PD first. Since I have a sufficient reason not to defect – *i.e.* it flouts AC – and “not defecting” is equivalent to cooperating, I acquire a sufficient reason to cooperate, which is therefore the rational thing to do. In the NP, on the other hand, having a sufficient reason not to two-box does not amount to a sufficient reason to one-box: “not two-boxing” is compatible with "one-boxing" as well as "no-boxing". So, to argue that B is rational we have to provide another reason. However, such a reason does not exists, as a decision to one-box is self-defeating. 
Indeed, a decision to one-box requires, as Walker argues, a second-order decision “to decide to one box”.  This decision provides reliable evidence of the unalterable presence of the  \$1,000,000 in the opaque box. However, since I prefer  \$1,001,000 to  \$1,000,000, I should expect to collect \$1,001,000 by two-boxing. «The reason to decide to one-box evaporates for anyone who forms the second order decision to do so.» Concluding, Walker argues that cooperation, by contrast to two-boxing, turns out to be rational even in a slightly modified PD in which not defecting is not equal to cooperating. The two problems, then, are proved to be essentially different.
